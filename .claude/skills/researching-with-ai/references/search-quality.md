# Search quality and evidence rules

## Query planning

For a complex question, create distinct queries for core facts, current state, primary sources, counter-evidence or risks, alternate-language material when useful, and social discussion only when relevant. Reject synonym-only duplicates.

## Source priority

Prefer primary materials: official documentation, original research, government or standards publications, project repositories, and first-party data. Then consider reputable specialist reporting and well-scoped community evidence. Treat aggregators, scraped copies, SEO pages, and unattributed summaries as weak evidence.

Do not rank solely by domain. Consider relevance, recency, authority, independence, conflicts of interest, direct support for the claim, and diversity across sources.

## Evidence handling

- Canonicalize and deduplicate URLs before counting corroboration.
- Distinguish multiple reports of one upstream story from independent confirmation.
- Use cleaned page evidence when possible; do not present a search-result snippet as the source text.
- Keep publication time separate from retrieval time.
- Label social posts and unverified community claims.
- Preserve useful partial evidence when a page is dead or a Provider fails.

## Citation checks

Before presenting an answer, verify that every marker resolves to a real returned source and that the nearby claim is supported. Flag uncited material claims, dangling markers, dead sources, citation drift, and excessive dependence on one source.

For high-stakes medical, legal, financial, or safety questions, use current authoritative sources and state that the output is research assistance rather than professional advice.
